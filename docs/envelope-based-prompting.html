Envelope-Bounded Retry with Typed Failure Ledgers for Unattended LLM Pipelines

Swaraj Shekhar
In short The problem: unattended LLM pipelines often need their prompt fixed mid-run, and a rewritten prompt is logic nobody has reviewed. The idea: the system may only choose among plans a human approved once per envelope version, and its feedback to itself is a short list of typed template messages rather than a growing transcript. What is claimed: three controller properties that hold by construction. What is not claimed: better output quality. This is an idea to build on, not a finished system or a tested method. Section 10.4 lists where others can contribute.
Abstract Unattended LLM pipelines fail in ways a person would normally fix by editing the prompt. If the system rewrites its own prompt, the rewrite is new logic that no one has reviewed. This paper restricts runtime behavior to choosing among plans that humans approve once per envelope version. Failures become typed, template-based directives, and a per-record fuse and a fleet-level breaker bound each record and each batch. The controller guarantees bounded prompt length, bounded termination, and containment of the plan, note, and invariant layers. These hold whatever the model does and say nothing about output quality. A pilot on receipt field extraction (CORD v1, 100 clean train records, qwen3-8k at temperature 0.3) confirms containment (H3) by construction; the controller's own prompt length is bounded (388 tokens at attempt 1, 506–653 at attempt 2), but the conversational retry baseline required by H1's definition was not run. All six dead-lettered records (6%) fail identically across three seeds on both attempts under envelope v2 — zero rescue events — revealing that retry effectiveness depends entirely on whether the failure-directive templates are specific enough to guide the model past the failure. Root-cause analysis of the six dead letters led directly to a revised envelope (v3); in v3 block 1 the controller accepted 100/100 records with 5–7 rescue events per seed, with 5 core records rescued in all three seeds. The sixth formerly dead-lettered record (train-00065) passes on attempt 1 in v3 because the increased output budget resolves truncation without retry. The v3 naive_retry baseline shows zero rescues on the core 5 systematic records in all seeds. The pilot provides evidence consistent with H2 (template specificity controls rescue effectiveness), but the formal ablation baselines (check-IDs-only vs. full-output directive) were not run, so H2 as defined is not confirmed. A McNemar exact test on the held-out test split (controller vs. naive_retry, 84 clean records) gives p = 0.0625–0.125 across seeds, borderline and not significant at α = 0.05. The validator accept rate (96.4% on test split) overstates field accuracy: reference-judge sampling found discrepancies in 14/14 sampled accepted records (Wilson 95% CI: [78.5%, 100%]), confirming that assurance sampling is necessary for any accuracy claim. Train-split reference-judge discrepancy rate was 78.6% (v2, Wilson 95% CI: [52.4%, 92.4%]) and 71.4% (v3, [45.4%, 88.3%]); human-review rate was 57.1% ([32.6%, 78.6%]).

1. Introduction

Self-correction in LLM systems usually follows a conversational pattern such as Reflexion [1]: the output, the error log, and a retry request are appended to the dialogue. This suits chat, where a person reads each turn. It fits poorly the systems this paper targets, which have three properties. The task is issued as a program-generated prompt, and the consumer of the output is code. The resident data payload D (documents, schemas, rule sets, retrieved passages) is large, often large enough that one raw failure trace can fill a significant part of the window. And no human is in the synchronous path: humans see records the system cannot resolve, and a sample of resolved ones, through an asynchronous queue.

In this regime, runtime prompt change is the central risk, because a rewritten prompt has not been reviewed in its final form. Three failure modes follow.

Context saturation: appended failure history consumes the budget that D needs, and each retry is longer than the last.

Attention attenuation: long-context models are weakest on information placed mid-prompt [2], so invariants go at the start and a reminder at the end. Recency override is a separate risk that this paper does not evaluate.

Rogue revision: an unconstrained rewrite can override an invariant, drift from the task, or repeat a failing approach. Without a bound on what may change, the only safeguard is a human reviewing each rewritten prompt, which defeats unattended operation.

The paper's contributions are:

(i) an approved envelope that bounds all runtime behavior to pre-approved plans and templates;

(ii) a typed, deduplicated failure ledger whose size depends on the envelope, not on the number of attempts;

(iii) a per-record fuse and a fleet-level breaker, with a termination argument for the controller;

(iv) an oversight model that counts human effort per envelope version, exception, and sampled output; and

(v) two-sided boundary enforcement, with input-side checks on plans and output-side validation of results. The termination and containment arguments are short because they follow from the controller's structure. Their value is that they hold regardless of model behavior, not that they are difficult to prove.

H2 and H4 are falsifiable hypotheses. H1 and H3 are checks of properties the design enforces. Section 9 specifies the tests.

  • H1 (prompt length, a baseline comparison). The length bound Lmax of Section 5.1 holds by construction, so H1 concerns only the baseline: conversational retry's prompt length grows with each attempt, and the layered design's does not.
  • H2 (invariant adherence). Adherence to invariants at the final attempt is higher under the layered design than under two baselines that differ in how much failure information they carry: one carries only check IDs and validator messages, the other the full failing output (Section 9.7 describes what these baselines require). H2 may be falsified: withholding failing output may reduce repair ability.
  • H3 (containment, a conformance check). Every executed prompt lies inside the approved envelope. This is enforced and checked for each prompt, not estimated; it is a property to verify, not an outcome to measure.
  • H4 (oversight load). On the test split, the fraction h of records needing human attention is below a target h*, fixed before the test split is used. No h* was established in this pilot; the observed oversight loads are reported in Section 9.3 and Section 9.7 describes what a formal H4 test would require.

Where this applies. The design suits tasks where a validator can decide pass or fail deterministically, the output is consumed by code, the data payload is large, and records can wait for an occasional human. It fits poorly where the check is a judgment only people can make, where each record needs a conversational exchange, or where an envelope cannot be written for the task family in advance. Envelope authoring is the main cost (L1).

The distinct elements of this paper are

(a) a separation of immutable and mutable prompt parts;

(b) a bounded failure memory that is typed, indexed by template group, and never written by a model;

(c) a termination rule combining attempt limits, failure-signature and plan repetition, and envelope membership; and

(d) approval once per envelope version, rather than review of each plan. Element (c) is the narrowest, since the graph papers already provide budgets and absorbing states.

3. Design Principles and Assumptions

Five commitments shape the design in Section 4. Invariants and allowed structure are stored as checkable objects (schemas, rule IDs, step IDs), with natural language used only where it cannot be formalized. Failures are compiled into directives chosen from a fixed template library, never into transcripts. Deterministic validators decide pass or fail. Every loop has attempt and time limits, so termination does not depend on the model stopping. Humans approve the set of allowed structures once per version and handle exceptions and samples; no runtime prompt waits for individual approval. Terms that change behaviour are defined in Appendix B.

3.1 Assumptions

The guarantees in Section 5 hold under the following assumptions. Each is a property the deployment must check before a run.

  1. A1. Window. The context window holds every block of Ψi at its envelope cap, so Lmax fits. A record whose D exceeds Dmax is rejected at intake, before any model call, and is logged as a rejection, not a dead letter.
  2. A2. Templates. Every validator check ID maps to exactly one directive group in T.
  3. A3. Satisfiable envelope. At least one plan in Papproved passes the approval lint (Section 5.3). The drift threshold satisfies θdrift ∈ [0, 1], and the repetition limit satisfies m ≥ 1.
  4. A4. Fallback menu. The fallback menu is finite, with K items, each tried at most once per record (Section 5.5).
  5. A5. Sandbox. Generated SQL and code run only in a sandbox with no network access and no write access to production state.
  6. A6. Side effects. External writes happen only after acceptance and are keyed by record ID. The first accepted result is written; later accepted results, including fallback results, are logged and not written.
  7. A7. Pinned versions. The executor, judge, and plan compiler are each pinned to a version recorded in the envelope. Changing any of them, including switching to a different model, is an envelope change requiring approval.
  8. A8. Validator scope. The validator covers the checks in T. Failures outside its coverage are seen by the fuse and breaker only through assurance sampling, and only to the extent sampling detects them (Section 7).
  9. A9. Review capacity. Reviewers clear the dead-letter queue within a review delay target fixed in the envelope, measured from dead-lettering to resolution. Otherwise records wait, and the delay is reported.
  10. A10. Initial plan. The first plan S1 is a designated member of Papproved named in the envelope.
  11. A11. Failure class. The failure class of a record is the directive group of its failing check. No separate classifier is used.
  12. A12. Judge scope. An LLM judge, if used, runs only on sampled accepted outputs, offline. It never runs in the per-record loop and never changes pass or fail.
  13. A13. Call timeout. Every executor, plan-compiler, and fallback call has a timeout set in the envelope. A call that times out counts as a failed attempt. Termination depends on this assumption, since the elapsed-time check runs only between calls.

4.1 Components

  • Invariant layer I. Output schema, hard rules, security guardrails, and a compact checklist of critical rules. Part of the envelope; never produced by the compiler.
  • Declared step graph G0. A finite set of step IDs V0, a precedence relation E0, and a mandatory subset M ⊆ V0, stating the allowed structure of execution for the task family.
  • Data payload D. Byte-identical across attempts, so every attempt sees the same data and audits can reproduce it. Wrapped in tags and treated as data, never instructions (Section 6.2). Not tested against an adversarial injection set in this pilot.
  • Strategy Si = (Pi, xi). Pi is a plan drawn from Papproved, a schema-constrained object whose steps are IDs from V0 or flagged extensions. xi is a note of at most smax tokens, built only from directive template text.
  • Ledger Ci. Active directives derived from failures (Section 4.3).
  • Validator V. Produces pass or fail and the set of failing check IDs Fi.
  • Plan compiler. Maps (Si, Ci, Fi) to Si+1 by choosing one plan from Papproved. It can be a deterministic policy over failure groups or an LLM call that sees only the ledger, failure signature, current plan, and template IDs. It never sees D or the raw output and cannot change the envelope.

4.2 Prompt Layout

Stable parts come first and mutable parts last. The system role carries the invariants I. The user message carries D, the task Q, the ledger, the plan, the note, and a short reminder R of the critical invariants, which sits near the end where attention is strongest. Within one envelope version, the stable prefix is identical on every attempt of a record, which also makes it cache-friendly; a fallback under another version uses that version's invariant block; prompt-caching cost comparison was not measured in this pilot. Appendix A.3 shows a complete instance.

4.3 Failure Ledger

  • Typed record. fi = (attempt, Fi, group, evidence-ref). The evidence reference points to a stored artifact, not to a copy inside the prompt.
  • Template directives. Each directive is drawn from the template library T, indexed by directive group. The compiler selects a template and one value from its declared parameter set. It never writes free-form directives. A note has the same form: a template ID with a declared parameter value.
  • One directive per group. Each check maps to exactly one group (A2), and the ledger holds at most one active directive per group. A new failure in a group replaces the old directive. Two directives for one group cannot coexist. Directives in different groups can still conflict (Section 10.2, Q3). A directive stays active until its group is replaced by a new failure or the record reaches a terminal state, even after the check it addresses passes. A satisfied directive therefore costs length and can over-constrain; whether to drop it on pass is an open choice.
  • Bounded size. |Ci| ≤ G · ℓmax, where G is the number of directive groups fixed in the envelope and ℓmax the directive length cap in tokens. The bound depends on the envelope, not on the number of attempts.

4.4 Envelope and Governance

The envelope 𝓔 = (I, G0, Papproved, T, Θ, length bounds) is what humans approve. Papproved is the finite list of approved plans, T the directive template library, and Θ the thresholds. The following rules govern it.

  • Versioned and signed. Each version is serialized canonically, hashed, and signed. The runtime verifies the signature at the start of a batch and for each record. A mismatch halts execution.
  • Two-person approval. A new version needs sign-off from two designated reviewers.
  • Separation of duties. The plan compiler and executor read the envelope and cannot write it.
  • Shadow before live. A new strategy family or threshold runs on a sample of records in shadow mode, with outputs logged but not returned, before it is enabled.
  • Approval lint. Before signing, every plan in Papproved is checked against the declared graph (Section 5.3). Drift is therefore measured at approval, not at runtime.
  • Pause and rollback. When the fleet breaker opens, the batch pauses. While paused, the batch processes no records. Resuming under the same or the last approved envelope version, and moving to any newer version, each require a recorded human decision.
  • Reproducible audit. Each executed prompt is stored by hash with the envelope version, record ID, and attempt index, so any executed prompt can be regenerated.

5. Formalization

5.1 State and Prompt Bound

The prompt at attempt i is the tuple below, where Q is the fixed task string and R the invariant reminder.

Ψi = (I, D, Si, Ci, Q, R)

Each block has a token cap fixed in the envelope, so the length is bounded as

|Ψi| ≤ Imax + Dmax + Pmax + smax + Qmax + Rmax + G ℓmax = Lmax

The total is independent of the attempt index. The assembler checks the bound before sending; a prompt that exceeds it is not sent, and the fuse opens (Section 5.4). Records with D above Dmax are rejected at intake (A1).

5.2 Two-Sided Boundary Enforcement

  • Input-side envelope check. Env(Si, 𝓔) = 1 only if the plan is in Papproved, satisfies the hard constraints of Section 5.3, and the note xi is an instance of a template in T. It uses no model: each factor is a lookup. It guards against a compiler fault or a mutated strategy.
  • Output-side validator. V(yi, I) → (pass, Fi) checks the output against the checks in T. Output correctness is guaranteed only to the extent those checks cover it (A8). Env cannot substitute for V.

Invariants that cannot be written as lookups are enforced by V.

5.3 Structural Constraints and Approval Lint

Drift is measured between the declared graph G0 = (V0, E0) and a plan P = (V, E). Both are discrete objects; no graph is extracted from natural language. A malformed plan is rejected as a failure, because the compiler emits plans under a schema.

Two hard constraints apply to every plan. Mandatory steps: M ⊆ V. Precedence: for every (u, v) ∈ E0 with u, v ∈ V, the plan orders u before v.

Similarity is the Jaccard index over step IDs, computed once per plan at approval:

Jstruct(G0, P) = |V ∩ V0||V ∪ V0|

Steps in V ∖ V0 are extensions. They must be flagged, and each reduces Jstruct. A plan is signed into Papproved only if it satisfies both hard constraints and Jstruct ≥ θdrift. At runtime the compiler selects only signed plans, so drift is a property of the approved list. Which list to approve is open (Section 10.2, Q2).

Figure 2: Worked drift example. The plan drops the mandatory validation step and adds an extension, retry. Here Jstruct = |V ∩ V0| / |V ∪ V0|, with the matched steps parse and write. The mandatory-step constraint also fails, so the approval lint rejects the plan.
Why a declared graph Extracting graphs from free text makes the drift measure depend on extraction noise. Requiring declared step IDs makes it exact and reproducible. The cost is that the graph must be authored per task family, which is part of envelope approval.

5.4 Repetition and the Per-Record Fuse

Two repetition signals are checked at fixed points in the loop, both on the record's own history. The failure signature σ = hash(sort(F)) is computed from the set of failing check IDs after each failure. It captures repeated check-level failures; it does not compare wording. The plan repeat count ri is computed for the candidate plan of attempt i before that attempt runs: it counts the earlier attempts in the record that used the same plan. Plans come from a finite list, so the comparison is exact. Plans that recur, including cycles such as A → B → A, are blocked by the count only once a plan has been used m times before. The smallest setting of m blocks any repeat; larger settings allow cycles to recur up to the attempt limit.

The per-record fuse is tested before each attempt, on the plan compiled for that attempt. Figure 1 draws it earlier for brevity, since the envelope tier needs the compiled plan. The fuse is the OR of three tiers, with Nmax and tmax the attempt and wall-clock limits and m the repetition count that trips the tier. CB(i) = 1 ("open") when attempt i is about to run, or when the failure that preceded it has just been recorded.

CB(i) =
1 if i > Nmax, or the elapsed time exceeds tmax (limit tier)
1 if the failure count for the current signature σ is at least m, or ri ≥ m (repetition tier)
1 if Env(Si, 𝓔) = 0, or the assembled prompt exceeds the caps of Section 5.1 (envelope tier)
0 (closed) otherwise

The elapsed-time check runs before every call, including fallback calls, and each call is bounded by the timeout of A13. Because the attempt limit is finite, the repetition tier acts only on the attempts it can still affect: a failure signature whose count reaches m stops the record at once, and a plan already used m times is not run again. The setting of m = 2 is used throughout; experimental calibration of m was not performed in this pilot (Section 9.7).

The fuse is a fuse, not a circuit breaker: once open, it stays open for the record, and no half-open probing is defined. When it opens, the record leaves the main loop and enters the fallback menu (Section 5.5). If the fallback menu is exhausted, the record is dead-lettered for human review. Fallback calls are outside the main loop. Only the elapsed-time tier and the length check of Section 5.1 apply to them. The attempt-index and repetition tiers do not, since fallback calls are not attempts and each item runs once (A4).

5.5 Fallback, Fleet-Level Breaker, and Termination

Fallback. The fallback menu has K items, tried in order: a prior approved envelope version (one executor call under that version) or a deterministic default (no model call). Each item is tried at most once per record. A fallback that passes validation is accepted. A fallback that fails moves the record to the next item. When the menu is exhausted, the record goes to the dead-letter queue. Fallback never re-enters the main loop, so a record makes at most K fallback calls.

Fleet breaker. Per-record fuses handle hard individual records. A systemic problem, such as a faulty template or a changed upstream schema, appears as a rising dead-letter rate or as silent failures in sampled outputs. These two signals use different windows. Let yr ∈ {0, 1} indicate whether record r was dead-lettered, over a sliding dead-letter window Wd. The silent-failure signal is read from an assurance window Wa of accepted outputs, sampled with the cluster design of Section 7. Let φ be the dead-letter fraction that trips the breaker, and φs the silent-failure rate over the window that trips it.

CBfleet(t) =
1 (pause batch and alert) if 1Wd Σr ∈ Wd(t) yr > φ, or
the lower confidence bound at level γ on the silent-failure rate in Wa(t) exceeds φs
0 otherwise

Using the lower confidence bound, rather than the point estimate, avoids pausing on sampling noise. The breaker pauses the batch rather than probing half-open, and resumption requires a human decision.

Proposition (termination of the controller). Under A3 and A4, with finite Nmax, tmax, and fallback menu size K, every record ends in acceptance or in the dead-letter queue. The number of model calls per record is at most 2Nmax + K: at most Nmax main-loop executor calls, at most Nmax plan-compiler calls, and at most K fallback calls. The judge is not in the per-record loop (A12), so it adds no calls to this bound.

Proof. The limit tier stops any main-loop call with index i > Nmax, so the main loop has at most Nmax executor calls, each paired with at most one compiler call after a failure. Each fallback item is tried once (A4), and its outcomes lead only to acceptance, the next item, or the absorbing dead-letter queue. The fallback cannot re-enter the main loop, so it is not a repair edge in the sense of Bounded Loops [14], and no cycle avoids the terminal outcomes. The breaker only shortens a batch. □

5.6 Containment and Human Oversight

A prompt is rogue if

(a) its invariant block differs from an approved envelope version;

(b) its plan is not in Papproved;

(c) its note or plan is not drawn from the approved vocabularies; or

(d) its length exceeds the envelope bound. Containment in this sense covers the invariant, plan, and note layers only. Content inside D is outside it, and is handled by the validator and by datamarking (Section 6.2).

Proposition (containment). Every prompt the controller sends is non-rogue, provided the envelope signature verifies, the envelope and length checks of Section 5.4 pass for every attempt, and assumptions A1, A2, and A7 hold. Conditions (a), (b), and (d) are enforced mechanically; condition (c) is enforced by the closed-vocabulary rule of Section 6.2. The residual risk lies in the wording of the template library, which is reviewed at approval, and in the coverage of V (Sections 8 and 10).

Oversight load follows. Let u be the fraction of all records still unresolved after fallback, and s the assurance sampling rate among accepted records. The fraction needing human attention is

h = u + s (1 − u)

A system without an approved envelope must treat each runtime-mutated prompt as potentially rogue. If a fraction pmut of records is mutated at runtime, reviewing every mutated prompt gives hbase = pmut. A fairer baseline reviews a random sample of mutations at the rate needed for the same detection power. That comparison was not made in this pilot (Section 9.7). When u is small, h ≈ u + s, so the envelope design's review load is set by the unresolved fraction and the sampling rate, while the review-everything load is set by pmut. The comparison is structural; no values are assumed here. This figure excludes the amortized per-version review effort RevE/|B| (records per envelope version) and fleet pause time, which are reported separately.

What humans approve Humans approve envelopes and their versions, not individual runtime prompts. Review effort scales with envelope changes, dead-letter records, and samples, not with the number of mutations.

6. Concerns Specific to Large Context

6.1 Attention and Stability

Middle-positioned content is less reliable in long prompts [2], so the invariant layer sits in the system role and a short reminder closes the prompt (Section 4.2). The reminder is a checklist with a fixed cap Rmax, not a copy of I. Positional breakdown of invariant adherence (mid-prompt vs. end-reminder) was not measured in this pilot. Each attempt receives the full data payload, so no attempt works from a partial view. D must be byte-stable across attempts; timestamps, random identifiers, and reordered retrieval results must be fixed upstream.

6.2 Data as an Attack Surface

Prompt structure alone gives no guarantee against injection [17] [18]. In autonomous pipelines, D can contain text that looks like instructions, drawn from documents, emails, or web pages. Two rules follow. Data stays data: D is wrapped in explicit tags with datamarking or spotlighting [19], and the prompt states that content inside data tags is never an instruction. Closed vocabulary: notes are template instances and plans are IDs in Papproved, so no span of D can enter a plan, a note, or the ledger. Env rejects anything outside these sets, and a rejected attempt opens the fuse. Directives come from template and check IDs, so a malicious payload cannot write a directive through the failure path.

The output validator (Section 8) is the backstop: every non-null text field must appear verbatim in D, and every typed field must equal, after a declared normalizer in T, a value in D. This catches invented values but not an injected value that does appear in the document. That residual is a known gap; an adversarial injection test was not included in this pilot. Injected content that passes both checks is a known gap, not a covered case.

6.3 Failures That Are Not Prompt Failures

A failing output may come from retrieval rather than from the instruction, for example when a required fact is absent from D. The validator alone does not separate these cases. In this version every record that exhausts its attempts and its fallback menu is dead-lettered, whatever the cause. Classification and retrieval repair are open (Section 10.2, Q4).

7. Operating With Minimal Human Interaction

Removing the human does not make a system autonomous; the design gives humans defined work. A dead-lettered record carries its attempts, failure signatures, ledger, last output, the check that opened the fuse or breaker, the input reference, and the envelope version, so a reviewer can act without reconstructing the run. Before a human sees a record, the fallback menu of Section 5.5 is tried. If the menu is exhausted, the record waits for review, and the rest of the batch continues. If dead-lettering exceeds the fleet threshold within the window, the batch pauses and an alert carries the failure signatures and a sample of records. Resumption is an explicit human decision about a systemic problem.

Assurance sampling. Accepted outputs, including those accepted on the first attempt, are sampled inside each assurance window. Sampling covers all accepted outputs without strata, so a failure concentrated in one template can be diluted by the rest; the cluster design below limits this. Sampling is the only way to detect silent failures that pass the validator. For a window with silent-failure rate at least ε, the chance that n independent samples all pass is at most (1 − ε)n. Choosing n ≥ ln(α) / ln(1 − ε) makes that chance at most α. The bound says how many samples detect a rate of ε; it does not estimate the rate.

Clustered failures. Silent failures often come in clusters, from one template, one batch, or one upstream change, so the independence bound above is a reference case, and the design below applies. The sampling unit is the cluster, defined as a directive group within a batch. Accepted outputs with no directive form one cluster per batch. Two regimes are distinguished. Cluster-level failures: a fraction εc of clusters is wrong, and every output in a wrong cluster fails. The window samples c clusters and misses every wrong cluster with probability at most (1 − εc)c, so c ≥ ln(α) / ln(1 − εc). Within each sampled cluster, a fixed number j of outputs is reviewed; drawing more outputs from one cluster adds little to cluster-level detection. Within-cluster failures: failures are spread across clusters, and outputs in one cluster are correlated with intraclass correlation ρ. The number of reviewed outputs must then be inflated by the design effect 1 + (k − 1)ρ, where k is the cluster size, to keep the power of the independent bound. ρ is not known, and was not estimated in the pilot (Section 9.7). The values of α, ε, εc, c, j, and n are set in the evaluation protocol.

External side effects happen only after acceptance (A6). Generated SQL and code run only in a sandbox (A5). The primary oversight metric is h, reported with the assurance-sample error rate and total reviewer minutes per 1,000 records. The audit log stores prompt hashes and outputs. Retention, access control, and redaction of personal data in D are deployment requirements, outside the scope of this paper.

Risk: the exception queue becomes the hidden workload If the dead-letter rate is high, human effort moves from synchronous review to queue triage. Total delay can exceed what the record count suggests, so reviewer time must be reported with the record count.

8. Enforcing the Immutable Boundary

No single mechanism is treated as sufficient. API role separation places I with provider-level priority, but it is not a guarantee against injection. Tag isolation with escaping keeps D distinguishable from instructions. The declared step graph with a schema-constrained plan gives the exact structural check (Section 5.3). Constrained decoding, as in Outlines [10], LMQL [11], and XGrammar [12], makes output syntactically valid by construction, where a provider supports it. Its semantic effect is task-dependent: grammar constraints improved both syntactic and semantic accuracy in logical parsing [23], while a study of small models found that schema conformance removes structural failures and leaves a semantic gap that depends on scale [20]. Constraints can also distort the output distribution when feasible probability mass is low, and a draft-conditioned variant reports gains in strict accuracy [24]. None of these is evaluated here. Guardrail classifiers such as NeMo Guardrails [25] and Llama Guard [26] are a content and policy layer complementary to V. The output validator V is the correctness gate, and Env is the input defense on plans. An LLM judge, if used, is advisory and runs only on samples (A12).

9. Pilot Experiment: Receipt Extraction on CORD v1

This section reports a pilot study on the CORD v1 dataset [27], evaluating the controller against single-shot and naive-retry baselines on receipt field extraction. The pilot covers envelope versions v2 and v3. All code is versioned, all envelopes are hashed and approved, and every run database is retained. This is a development pilot, not a definitive evaluation: it uses one task family, includes a held-out test split, and omits conditions that require full baseline implementation (conversational retry, Reflexion).

9.1 Dataset and Task

CORD v1 (CC BY 4.0) is a receipt understanding dataset of 800 train, 100 validation, and 100 test records, each an annotated image of a retail receipt. Input is text reconstructed from word-level bounding-box annotations; no image or OCR output is used. The task is to extract nine total-related amount fields (total_price, subtotal_price, tax_price, service_price, discount_price, cashprice, changeprice, creditcardprice, emoneyprice) and a menu list (nm, cnt, price per item) from each receipt text.

The validator checks that each non-null amount, once stripped of currency symbols and thousands separators, corresponds to a number that appears verbatim in the input text. Menu item names are checked for verbatim substring presence. This validator is intentionally permissive: it allows any numeric value that appears in the text, even if it is the wrong field. Arithmetic consistency checks are not implemented, so a record can pass the validator while containing a field extracted from the wrong line.

A clean record is one where every CORD reference value appears verbatim in the input text, making the task well-posed. Of 800 train records, 614 are clean. Pilot runs use the first 100 clean records (offset 0, limit 100) and a calibration block of 400 more (offset 100, limit 400).

9.2 Envelope Versions and Conditions

Envelope v2 uses qwen3-8k:latest (Q4_K_M, served by Ollama on a remote GPU machine, model digest a68fd3999784d0ff878cb799bfe1a6f19f0ddf405651728609d687604ce10955), num_ctx=8192, num_predict=512, temperature=0.3, Nmax=3, m=2. Templates describe each field with a "copy exactly as printed" phrasing. Three plans (A, B, C) are approved; plan C adds disambiguation text for payment fields. Envelope v2 was approved and hashed before any run.

Envelope v3 raises num_predict to 1024 and strengthens all templates: the menu directive now specifies "the last printed number for each item" (addressing @ unit-price ambiguity) and all amount templates now say "NOT the field label" (addressing label contamination). V3 was created after root-cause analysis of v2 dead letters and was approved before v3 runs begin. The envelope change is a governance event: new version, new hash, new approval record.

Conditions: (1) controller — the full ledger-based retry loop (Nmax=3, m=2); (2) single_shot — Nmax=1, one attempt per record; (3) naive_retry — Nmax=3, same prompt on every attempt (no ledger directives). The single_shot and naive_retry baselines use the same envelope, model, and temperature as the controller. All three conditions are run with seeds {0, 1, 2} in block 1 and on the test split. Code, envelope files, and per-run SQLite databases are available at the repository accompanying this paper; the CORD v1 dataset is available under CC BY 4.0 at huggingface.co/datasets/naver-clova-ix/cord-v1.

9.5 Envelope Versioning: v2 → v3

Following the root-cause analysis, a revised envelope v3 was authored, hashed, and approved. Changes: (1) num_predict raised 512→1024 to accommodate long menu lists; (2) the menu template now specifies "price is the LAST printed number on the item line — never the @ unit-price or both numbers"; (3) all nine amount templates now say "extract ONLY the amount value, NOT the field label." No code changes were required. The envelope governance mechanism — new version, new hash, human approval — forces the template change to be deliberate, documented, and auditable.

This is the intended operating model: the controller surfaces structural failures that the template cannot address, a human updates the template, and the new envelope governs all subsequent runs.

9.7 What Was Not Measured

The following quantities from the protocol were not measured in this pilot and remain open. The repetition count m=2 is used throughout; experimental calibration was not performed. The within-cluster correlation ρ needs a larger batch with clustered failure data. Per-version review effort RevE was not timed. The conversational retry (Reflexion-style) and the H2 ablation baselines (check-IDs-only vs. full-output) are not implemented in this pilot. Fuse precision and recall require counterfactual runs.

What would formally confirm H2. H2 as defined in Section 1 requires demonstrating that the typed template content — not merely the presence of additional context — is what drives rescue. This requires two ablation baselines that were not run: (a) a check-IDs-only baseline that injects only the failure check identifier on attempt 2, without the template directive text; and (b) a full-output baseline that appends the raw previous output. Neither was implemented. The pilot's evidence is that naive_retry (same prompt repeated, no directive injection) never rescues the core 5 systematic failures across any seed, while the controller (full typed directive) rescues all five. This is consistent with H2 but does not isolate the typed template text as the active ingredient. A subsequent pilot with these baselines is needed to formally confirm H2.

10. Status, Open Questions, and Limitations

10.1 Status of the Claims

Enforced by the controller, by construction: bounded prompt length (Section 5.1), termination of the controller loop (Section 5.5), and containment of the invariant, plan, and note layers (Section 5.6). These hold whatever the model does, provided the envelope signature verifies and assumptions A1–A7 and A13 hold.

Confirmed in the pilot (Section 9): H3 (all dead-lettered records stopped by the repetition fuse inside the envelope, zero rogue prompts). H1 as defined in Section 1 compares prompt growth against a conversational retry baseline; that baseline was not run. What was measured: controller prompt tokens stay at 388 at attempt 1 and 506–653 at attempt 2, stable within num_ctx=8192 — the layered design's own prompt length is bounded by construction. Whether conversational retry would have grown past this is an open question; the test receipts are short, and the large-context claim is not stressed at this scale.

H2 — pilot evidence, not confirmed as defined: H2 as defined in Section 1 requires demonstrating that typed template directives, and not merely any additional context, drive rescue. The formal ablation baselines (check-IDs-only vs. full-output) were not run. The pilot provides consistent evidence: in v3 block 1, 5 core records are rescued in all three seeds; the v3 naive_retry baseline shows zero rescues on those same 5 records across all seeds (including the test split, where naive_retry DL sets are identical to single_shot in every seed — zero stochastic rescues). A McNemar exact test on the test split (controller vs. naive_retry on the same 84 records) gives p = 0.0625–0.125 across seeds, borderline and not significant at α = 0.05 two-tailed. The pattern is consistent with template specificity driving rescue effectiveness, but the pilot does not formally confirm H2 as stated.

H4 — observations on oversight load, target not pre-fixed: H4 specifies a pre-fixed oversight target h* that was not established before this pilot. Observed oversight loads: v2 h = 0.06, hoversight = 0.20, reference discrepancy rate 78.6% (Wilson 95% CI: [52.4%, 92.4%]); v3 h = 0.00, hoversight = 0.14, reference discrepancy 71.4% ([45.4%, 88.3%]), human-review discrepancy 57.1% ([32.6%, 78.6%]). Reference judge found 14/14 test-split sampled records had CORD annotation discrepancies (100%, Wilson 95% CI: [78.5%, 100%]), confirming the validator accept rate (96.4%) drastically overstates field accuracy. The human review is more authoritative than the reference judge; the validator is permissive and assurance sampling is necessary for any accuracy claim.

Fleet breaker calibrated: φ = 0.120, Wd = 50 (v2 block 2, p₀ = 0.0625).

Not yet measured: conversational retry comparison (H2 formal baselines), frozen-strategy ablation, fuse precision/recall, drift–failure correlation, per-version reviewer time, within-cluster ρ.

Not claimed: output correctness (validator is permissive; 57–79% of accepted train records showed reference or human-review discrepancies; 100% of sampled test records showed reference discrepancies), and whether the approved plans are good choices for a record (L2).

10.2 Open Questions

These are research questions that the specification makes precise but does not answer.

  1. Q1. Selection versus a frozen strategy. Does choosing among Papproved, by a fixed rule or a learned selector, beat a frozen strategy? If the ablation matches, runtime plan choice contributes little and the envelope is the method.
  2. Q2. Size of the plan space. This paper uses a finite approved list, so drift is an approval-time lint. Synthesizing plans within G0 would make drift a runtime signal but make containment harder to claim. Which is better is open.
  3. Q3. Cross-group consistency. Two groups can each be satisfied while their combined requirements conflict. The invoice example has a typed date field and a verbatim provenance rule, which are compatible only through a declared normalizer. One directive per group does not detect such conflicts in general.
  4. Q4. Addressable failures. In the large-context regime, what fraction of failures can a prompt change fix, and what fraction reflect a fact missing from D?
  5. Q5. Silent-failure rates under clustering. How should a silent-failure rate be estimated when failures cluster by template or batch? The independent bound in Section 7 assumes independent samples within the window; the cluster design there does not estimate a rate.
  6. Q6. When envelope authoring pays. When is an envelope worth more than a fixed prompt with the same validator? Authoring time was not measured in this pilot; the comparison is an open empirical question.

10.3 Limitations

L1. Envelope authoring effort Guarantees hold relative to the envelope. A narrow envelope blocks legitimate adaptation, and a broad one permits behavior the task owner did not intend. Authoring and maintaining the envelope is the main effort of the approach.
L2. Selection among pre-approved plans; non-determinism Runtime behavior is selection among precomputed plans. The system cannot generate a strategy that no one approved, and the drift measure has no runtime role. A plan that passes the envelope check is legal but may still be a poor choice for a record; drift–failure correlation was not measured in this pilot (Section 9.7). Illegal plans fail Env and are dead-lettered. Re-execution does not make the model deterministic, so output quality varies across runs and model versions. The termination and containment properties concern the controller, not output quality.
L3. Residual cross-group conflicts One directive per group removes contradictions within a group but not across groups. Two groups can still issue conflicting directives, such as "be concise" and "include exhaustive detail." A new failure in a group also overwrites the previous directive for that group, which can drop a directive that still applies.
L4. Threshold sensitivity θdrift and m are domain-dependent, and φ and φs depend on the nominal rates assumed in their null model. A low drift threshold admits plans far from the declared graph; a high one rejects legitimate plans at approval. Calibration needs labeled data, which a new task may not have. A small m blocks repeated failure signatures and plans early, which may be too strict for some tasks.
L5. Silent failures, human delay, and hidden workload A weak validator passes flawed outputs, and neither the fuse nor the dead-letter count sees them; only sampled silent failures do. Samples are clustered as in Section 7, but the within-cluster correction requires ρ, which has not been estimated, so the design is not validated for correlated failures. Template wording is reviewed once, at approval. Dead-lettered records wait for reviewers, so throughput depends on the dead-letter rate. A low oversight load is not evidence of correctness; assurance sampling and reviewer-time reporting are needed for any claim of autonomy.

10.4 Where You Can Contribute

The idea is meant to be built on. Useful work includes:

  • Answering Q1 and Q2 on a real task family, comparing a runtime selector with a frozen strategy and checking how large the plan space can be before approval becomes impractical.
  • Measuring the quantities listed in Section 9.7: the repetition count m, the within-cluster correlation ρ, and per-version review effort RevE.
  • Proposing a failure-class or cross-group conflict check that addresses Q3 and L3.
  • Running the ablations and baselines described in Section 9.7 and reporting failures of H1–H4, not only successes.
  • Writing acceptance cases for Appendix D under the definitions in Appendix B.

11. Conclusion

Runtime prompt revision in large-context pipelines with minimal human interaction is effectively new program logic generated during execution, and reviewing each instance does not scale. This paper limits runtime behavior to choosing among plans that a human approves once per envelope version, and records failures in a bounded, typed ledger. Each record is governed by a fuse, and the batch by a fleet-level breaker. The controller's termination and containment properties hold regardless of model behavior. A pilot on CORD v1 receipt extraction confirms containment (H3) by construction and shows the controller's own prompt length stays bounded — the conversational baseline required by H1's definition was not run — and demonstrates the governance mechanism in action: root-cause analysis of the v2 dead letters led directly to a revised envelope (v3) — changes that previously required debugging a live system now require authoring a new envelope version, hashing it, and obtaining human approval. The pilot surfaces a key practical lesson: the retry mechanism is only effective when the approved templates are specific enough to give the model new information on attempt 2. On the held-out test split, the controller achieves 96.4–97.6% accept rate (2–3 DL / 84 records) vs. 90.5–92.9% (6–8 DL) for single_shot and naive_retry; naive_retry DL sets are identical to single_shot in every seed (zero stochastic rescues), consistent with H2. The pilot does not formally confirm H2 or H4 as defined: the ablation baselines were not run and the oversight target h* was not pre-fixed. The validator accept rate overstates field accuracy; reference-judge sampling found discrepancies in 14/14 sampled test records. Assurance sampling and per-version review effort remain necessary for any correctness claim under unattended operation.

The trace is one case of the rule for a symbolic m. It holds for 1 < m ≤ 2: attempt 2 runs only if m is above 1, and the record stops at attempt 2 only if m is at most 2. Appendix B. Definitions of Behaviour-Affecting Terms

The terms below fix the behaviour of the design. They define the idea, not an implementation. Any realisation that uses these definitions and reproduces the traces in Appendix A makes the same decisions.

Record. One task instance with payload D and a stable record ID. All retry state (attempt index, failure history, plan history, ledger) belongs to one record and is discarded at its terminal state.

Batch. The set of records processed under one envelope version. The batch is the scope of the fleet breaker and the unit from which clusters are formed.

Attempt. One executor call on a record in the main loop, indexed i in order of execution. Fallback calls are not attempts.

Validator output. For each attempt, V evaluates every check in T that applies to the output. A failure does not stop evaluation, so the failing set Fi is complete. An output is accepted when Fi is empty.

Failure signature σ. A hash of the sorted set of failing check IDs in Fi. It depends only on check IDs, not on output text, position, or evidence. The failure count for σ is the number of attempts in the record's history whose signature equals σ.

Plan ID and repeat count. The plan ID identifies a member of Papproved. The repeat count ri is the number of earlier attempts in the same record whose plan ID equals the candidate's plan ID. Plan IDs are compared exactly.

Directive group. A set of validator checks that share one template in T. Each check belongs to exactly one group (A2), and the group is the failure class (A11).

Cluster. The pair of a batch identifier and a directive group. Accepted outputs with no directive form one cluster per batch. The cluster is the sampling unit of the clustered design in Section 7.

Assurance window Wa. The accepted outputs of one batch, taken in order of acceptance and grouped into consecutive blocks of a size fixed in the envelope. A block closes when it is full, and the assurance design of Section 7 is applied to each closed block.

Dead-letter window Wd. The most recent Wd records of the batch to reach a terminal state, in order of terminal state. Each record is counted once, at its terminal state.

Dead letter, rejection, and acceptance. A dead letter is a record whose fallback menu is exhausted without acceptance. A rejection is a record refused at intake because its payload exceeds Dmax; it makes no model call and is excluded from both windows. An accepted output is an output that passes V with an empty Fi, after the attempt or fallback that produced it.

Silent failure. An accepted output that a reviewer, on sampling, judges incorrect although V passed it.

Elapsed time. Wall-clock time from the start of the record's first executor call to the moment of the check. Time spent waiting for human review, and time during a batch pause, are excluded.

Batch pause. When the breaker opens, the batch issues no new record-level calls. Calls already in flight complete, and their results are logged. Their outputs are accepted only if the batch resumes after a recorded human decision.

Env check. The deterministic lookup of Section 5.2. It makes no model call and has no side effects.

Fallback menu. The ordered list attached to an envelope version (A4). Each item is either a prior approved envelope version or a deterministic default.

Breaker statistics. The dead-letter fraction over Wd, and the lower confidence bound at level γ on the silent-failure rate over the samples in closed assurance blocks. The level γ is fixed in the envelope.

Ledger update. When a failure in directive group g is recorded, the ledger entry for g is replaced by the template instance selected for that failure. Other entries are unchanged.

Appendix C. Component Contracts

Each component is specified by what it receives, what it returns, and what it may not read. No data formats are fixed here.

Validator V. Receives an attempt output and the templates in T. Returns pass or fail and the failing set Fi. It evaluates all applicable checks and reads no other input.

Plan compiler. Receives the current plan ID, the ledger, the failing set, and the plan and template IDs of the envelope. Returns a plan ID in Papproved and a note instance. It does not read D or the output, and it cannot modify the envelope.

Env check. Receives a plan ID, a note instance, and the envelope 𝓔. Returns 0 or 1. It makes no model call.

Ledger. Receives a failure record and returns the updated ledger. It holds at most one entry per directive group (Appendix B, ledger update).

Failure record. Holds the attempt index, the failing set, the failure signature, the directive group of each failing check, and a pointer to the stored evidence. It holds no copy of the output.

Dead-letter record. Holds the record ID, the attempts and their failure records, the ledger, a pointer to the last output, the check or tier that opened the fuse, and the envelope version.

Sampler. Receives the closed assurance blocks of a batch and their clusters. Returns the selected clusters and the outputs to review in each.

Breaker. Receives the terminal states in Wd and the samples in closed blocks. Returns pause or continue.
